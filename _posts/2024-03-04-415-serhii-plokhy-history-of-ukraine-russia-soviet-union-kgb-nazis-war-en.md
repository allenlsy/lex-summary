### **1. The Soviet Collapse: A Multilayered Process**

The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 was not a single event, but a convergence of three interrelated processes: the ideological unraveling of communism, the end of the Cold War, and the actual dissolution of the multinational state. These developments are often conflated, but they represent distinct historical trajectories.

The ideological collapse of communism occurred across the Soviet territory, not in isolation. It was not merely a shift in political rhetoric, but a deep cultural and intellectual crisis. The Soviet model, built on a centralized, state-controlled ideology, began to fail under pressure from economic stagnation, administrative inefficiency, and the erosion of moral authority. By the late 1980s, even long-standing party members began to question the legitimacy of the system.

The end of the Cold War, by contrast, refers to the broader geopolitical transformation that unfolded in the late 1980s. This was not a sudden victory of the West, but a gradual de-escalation of U.S.-Soviet rivalry. The most visible moment was the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989, but the deeper shift was in Moscow’s willingness to reduce military expenditures, accept political pluralism, and begin diplomatic normalization with the West.

However, the most profound and lasting transformation was the disintegration of the Soviet Union itself—a political and territorial collapse that redefined the modern map of Eastern Europe. This was not merely a political transition; it was a reconfiguration of national identities and state sovereignty. As Plokhy emphasizes, the Soviet Union did not vanish because of ideology alone. It dissolved because Kyiv, Minsk, and other major cities found themselves not in a new Soviet republic, but in newly independent states.

This point is critical. The Soviet Union had once been a superpower, controlling territory stretching from the Baltic to the Pacific, and housing over 300 million people across 15 republics. Its fall was not the result of a single policy or leader, but a cascading sequence of events: economic crisis, mass mobilization from below, and the weakening of central authority. Plokhy frames this as a "collapse from below"—a process driven by local actors, grassroots movements, and institutional breakdown, not a top-down design.

This analysis contrasts with common Western narratives that attribute the fall to U.S. pressure or Western diplomacy. While U.S. policy during the Cold War did shape Soviet strategic calculations, it did not drive the collapse. In fact, U.S. leaders, particularly President George H.W. Bush, were deeply alarmed by the breakup of the Soviet Union. The White House did not want a fragmented, unstable Russia. Bush’s visit to Kyiv in August 1991, just before the failed coup in Moscow, included a speech that explicitly warned Ukrainian leaders against pursuing independence—dubbed the "Chicken Kyiv" speech by analysts.

This intervention, far from encouraging sovereignty, was an effort to preserve the Soviet framework. U.S. officials were not rooting for the breakup; they were trying to manage it. The official rationale was to prevent a nuclear crisis, as the Soviet arsenal was still under Moscow’s control, and many feared that weapons might fall into the hands of rogue republics.

Moreover, the U.S. government had a vested interest in maintaining a stable, if subordinate, partner. Gorbachev, who had introduced reforms like glasnost and perestroika, was seen as a reliable interlocutor. His leadership was viewed as essential to managing the transition, not dismantling it. The Bush administration, facing a re-election campaign, was eager to highlight achievements in arms control, not the breakup of a global power.

Thus, the notion that the U.S. orchestrated the Soviet collapse is not supported by documentary evidence. The United States, far from seeking the end of the USSR, actively worked to keep it together—until November 1991, when it became evident that the process was irreversible.

### **2. Gorbachev and the Possibility of Alternative Paths**

Mikhail Gorbachev remains a central figure in the Soviet collapse. He introduced reforms that unintentionally dismantled the system. His vision of a "new thinking" in Soviet foreign policy, coupled with domestic reforms, created a vacuum. He intended to revitalize the Soviet system, not end it. But reform, in the context of a decaying empire, often leads to unintended consequences.

Plokhy argues that while history is not subject to counterfactuals, the question of "what if" remains instructive. Could the Soviet Union have survived under different conditions? Yes—though not in its current form.

There were several possible trajectories. One was the successful management of reforms—better communication, more coordinated economic policy, and a stronger strategy to contain nationalist movements. Another was a continuation of stagnation, with no reforms, leading to eventual collapse under economic and social strain. A third was a return to authoritarian control, like that under Brezhnev, but under more extreme conditions.

But Plokhy insists that the collapse was inevitable due to broader global forces. The 20th century was defined by the dismantling of empires. From the Ottoman Empire to the Austro-Hungarian, from the British to the French, empires had been fragmenting since the 19th century. The Soviet Union was not an exception. It was, in fact, the final major European empire.

The Russian Empire had collapsed in 1917. The Bolsheviks had temporarily reassembled it under a new name, but the ideological underpinning—internationalism—was unsustainable. By the late 1980s, the global shift toward national sovereignty, combined with economic failure, made the Soviet structure untenable.

Thus, Plokhy concludes: the Soviet collapse was not a surprise. It was the final chapter in a centuries-long story of imperial decay.

### **3. The Role of Ukraine in the Final Break**

The most decisive moment in the Soviet collapse came in December 1991, when Ukraine held a national referendum on independence. The question on the ballot was not whether Ukraine wanted to leave the Soviet Union, but whether it supported the decision of the Ukrainian Parliament (Verkhovna Rada) to declare independence.

This was a critical distinction. The referendum did not ask "Do you want Ukraine to become independent?" It asked, "Do you support the decision of the Ukrainian Parliament?" This limited framing was deliberate. It removed the issue from a general referendum and made it a ratification of a parliamentary act.

Yet, the outcome was decisive. Over 90% of voters supported the move.

One week later, the Soviet Union was formally dissolved. Russia, Belarus, and Ukraine all announced their separation. But the timing was not coincidence. The result was made possible by two factors.

First, Russia’s leadership, under Boris Yeltsin, recognized that Ukraine was too large and too vital to lose. Ukraine was the second-largest republic by population and economic potential. It was culturally and linguistically close to Russia, and its exit would leave Russia politically and demographically vulnerable.

Yeltsin himself told President Bush that without Ukraine, Russia would be outnumbered and outvoted by Muslim republics in the new Commonwealth of Independent States. This was not just a cultural concern—it was a strategic one.

Second, the Soviet economy was in ruins. Yegor Gaidar, Yeltsin’s chief economic advisor, told him bluntly: “We don’t have money to support the other republics. We must focus on Russia.” The Soviet state, once a machine of imperial dominance, had become a bankrupt entity.

The financial reality, combined with nationalist pressure, made the breakup unavoidable. Ukraine’s decision to leave was not a rebellion—it was a mathematical inevitability.

### **4. The Myth of the "Russian World" and Putin’s Narrative**

Vladimir Putin’s 2022 statement that the collapse of the Soviet Union was “the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the 20th century” has become a cornerstone of Russian war propaganda. Plokhy analyzes this claim not as a historical judgment, but as a political one.

Putin’s framing is not about lives lost—1991 was a low-cost transition in human terms. The price was not in bodies, but in identity. For Putin, the real tragedy was not the loss of soldiers or civilians, but the loss of empire and the perception of national unity.

This worldview reflects a deep-seated Russian imperial identity. For Putin, Russia is not a nation-state but a civilizational project. The idea that Russians and Ukrainians are “one people” dates back to the 19th century, when Russian imperial ideology categorized Ukrainians as “little Russians” and Belarusians as “white Russians.”

This narrative was revived by Putin in his 2021 essay, *On the Historical Unity of Russians and Ukrainians*. In it, he claims that Ukrainians, Russians, and Belarusians have always been part of a single nation. He argues that the idea of Ukrainian nationhood is a “delusion” created by external forces.

But Plokhy notes that this argument is not new. It echoes pre-1917 Russian imperial thought. It was also used to justify the 1917 revolution, which, while anti-imperial, failed to erase the notion of a unified Russian nation.

The essay was not just historical revisionism. It was a political instrument. It was published just months before the full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Its timing, combined with the rise of nationalist sentiment in Russia, made it a direct justification for war.

Moreover, Plokhy points out that the essay’s argumentation mirrors that of late 19th-century Russian imperial writers. It relies on a romanticized view of shared history, language, and religion—especially Orthodoxy.

But this narrative ignores key facts. The Ukrainian people, in the 20th century, developed a distinct national consciousness. This was not a Western imposition. It emerged from centuries of cultural resistance, including the Cossack uprisings, the 19th-century revival of Ukrainian literature, and the 1917 declaration of independence.

The myth of a “unified Russian world” has been used to justify not only the 2014 annexation of Crimea and the war in Donbas, but also the current invasion. The claim that Ukraine is a “fascist state” or a “Nazi state” is a deliberate distortion of history.

Plokhy rejects this outright. He notes that Ukraine’s far-right parties have never gained more than 2.15% of the vote in any election. In the 2019 parliamentary elections, the far-right party secured only 2.15%—a marginal result.

Even more telling, Ukraine is the only country outside Israel to have a Jewish president—Volodymyr Zelenskyy, who is, according to Plokhy, the most popular president in Ukrainian history.

Thus, the claim that Ukraine is a “Nazi state” is not only false—it is dangerous. It is a tool to delegitimize resistance and justify violence.

### **5. The Cossack Myth and the Rise of National Identity**

The Cossack myth plays a central role in Ukrainian nation-building. In the 1820s, a mysterious manuscript titled *The History of the Rus* began circulating in Russia. It was attributed to a dead Orthodox archbishop and claimed that the Cossacks were the original people of Rus—the ancestors of the Ukrainian nation.

This text was not a scholarly work, but a political instrument. It was read by key Russian intellectuals, including Alexander Pushkin and the Decembrist leader Mikhail Bestuzhev-Ryumin. It was also embraced by Ukrainian national revivalists in the 19th century.

The text made a radical claim: the Cossack elite were not just loyal to the Russian Empire—they were a separate nation. This was a direct challenge to the imperial model, which relied on elite integration through loyalty, not identity.

Plokhy argues that this was the turning point. Once the discourse shifted from loyalty to nation, empire could not survive. The Russian Empire had built itself on a system of elite integration—pacts with Poles, Muslims, Roman Catholics. But when identity replaced loyalty, the system failed.

The Cossack myth became the foundation of Ukrainian nationalism. It inspired poetry, folklore, and later, political movements. The Ukrainian national anthem includes the phrase, “Cossacks were the first to rise,” a direct reference to this myth.

The irony is that this myth, which was used to justify Ukrainian independence, was initially propagated by Russian authorities who wanted to integrate Ukrainian elites into the empire. But it backfired. The Cossack identity, once used to legitimize imperial rule, became a weapon of liberation.

### **6. Stepan Bandera: Hero, Symbol, and Controversy**

Stepan Bandera remains one of the most controversial figures in Ukrainian history. He was a student in interwar Ukraine, a region under Polish rule. He became a leader of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists (OUN), a radical nationalist group.

Bandera was arrested in 1938 and sentenced to death for organizing assassinations of Polish officials. During his trial, he delivered a defiant speech that made him a hero among Ukrainian youth. His sentence was commuted to life in prison.

When World War II began, Bandera was released by the Germans, who saw him as a potential ally. He then split the OUN into two factions: one radical, led by Bandera, and another more moderate.

In 1941, Bandera’s forces declared an independent Ukrainian state in Lviv. This was not authorized by the Germans. The Nazis arrested him, his family, and his brothers—two of whom died in Auschwitz.

Bandera was imprisoned in Sachsenhausen until 1944. He never returned to Ukraine. He lived in exile in Munich until his death in 1959.

Today, Bandera is a symbol of resistance. His popularity surged after February 24, 2022, when Russia invaded Ukraine. He was seen as a fighter against Soviet occupation.

But his legacy is contested. He worked with Nazi Germany, and his movement committed atrocities. Plokhy acknowledges this. However, he argues that Bandera was not a Nazi. He was a nationalist who sought independence. He refused to declare himself a fascist, and he refused to retract his declaration of Ukrainian independence.

The label “Nazi” was first used by Soviet propaganda and later adopted by Russian state media. Plokhy points out that Stalin also collaborated with Hitler in 1939. To call Bandera a Nazi, one must also call Stalin a Nazi.

Moreover, Bandera’s family was exterminated by the Nazis. He spent years in concentration camps. These facts undermine the one-dimensional portrayal of him as a fascist.

Plokhy concludes: Bandera is a myth. But a myth that reflects a real historical desire for national freedom.

### **7. The KGB: Power, Paranoia, and Legacy**

The KGB was not just a secret police force—it was a state within a state. During the 1950s and 1960s, it was weakened by internal struggles, particularly after the arrest of Lavrentiy Beria, who had been a dominant figure under Stalin.

But by the 1970s, under Yuri Andropov, the KGB regained power. Andropov himself was a former KGB chief, and he was part of the inner circle that made key decisions—like the invasion of Afghanistan.

The KGB had a dual role: internal surveillance and foreign operations. It controlled border troops, counterintelligence, and even the media. In the 1980s, it was more powerful than the military or the Communist Party in some ways.

Plokhy argues that the KGB’s culture shaped modern Russian leadership. Vladimir Putin, who served as a KGB foreign intelligence officer for 16 years, inherited this mindset.

The KGB taught its officers to think strategically, to plan long-term operations, and to manipulate information. It cultivated a sense of superiority over political leadership. Many KGB officers believed that the party was weak, corrupt, and ineffective.

This view was shared by Putin and his circle. When Putin came to power, he did not replace the KGB—he absorbed it. The institutions, the mindset, the culture of secrecy and control—all were preserved.

This is why Plokhy sees the war in Ukraine as a continuation of Soviet-era logic. It is not a new conflict. It is a return to imperial thinking.

### **8. The Road to War: Key Turning Points**

The war in Ukraine did not begin in 2022. It began in 2014, when Russia invaded Crimea.

The immediate trigger was Ukraine’s decision to sign a European Union Association Agreement. This was a major shift toward the West. But under pressure from Moscow, Ukraine’s President Viktor Yanukovych canceled the agreement.

This decision triggered mass protests in Kyiv. The government responded with violent crackdowns on peaceful demonstrators. The use of police violence—particularly against students—shocked the nation.

The protests became known as the Revolution of Dignity. They led to Yanukovych’s ousting and a new government.

Russia responded by seizing Crimea. The so-called "Green Men"—unmarked soldiers—appeared in Ukrainian cities. They were not Russian troops, but Russian special forces.

This was not a spontaneous action. It was a pre-planned military operation. The goal was to prevent Ukraine from joining NATO and the EU.

The war in Donbas followed. Russian-backed separatists declared independence in the Donbas region, which has a large ethnic Russian population. But Plokhy notes that before 2014, there was no mass mobilization of Russians in Donbas. The separatist movement was not a spontaneous uprising—it was orchestrated by Moscow.

The Donbas region is a classic industrial rust belt. It was built on coal mining and heavy industry. But after the fall of the Soviet Union, the mines closed. Unemployment soared. The region became a breeding ground for instability.

When Russia invaded, it used this economic despair to its advantage. It framed the war as a defense of Russian speakers. But Plokhy points out that Russian is not persecuted in Ukraine. Ukrainian schools exist throughout the country.

In fact, many Ukrainians in the east speak Russian as their first language. But they still identify as Ukrainian.

The war was not about language. It was about sovereignty.

### **9. Language, Identity, and the Future of Ukraine**

Before 2014, Ukrainian and Russian were roughly equal in use across the country. But the war shifted the balance. Crimea and parts of Donbas were lost. The population in those areas was displaced.

Today, Ukrainian is the dominant language in most of Ukraine. In the west, it is the only language. In the center and east, it is increasingly used.

Plokhy notes that Ukraine is the only truly bilingual country he has ever visited. In any city, you can speak in either language and be understood.

But since 2014, a generational shift has occurred. Younger people are choosing Ukrainian as a symbol of national identity. After February 24, 2022, many who had only spoken Russian began to use Ukrainian in public.

One anecdote: Plokhy spoke to a man in Geneva who had lived in Crimea. He had never spoken Ukrainian, but during a conversation, he switched to Ukrainian and said, “I don’t want to have anything in common with people who did this to us.”

This shift is not just linguistic—it is political and emotional.

Plokhy believes that Ukrainian will continue to rise in prominence. The war has created a new national consciousness. The state is no longer seen as a foreign occupier. It is now a protector.

### **10. The Future of the War: Scenarios and Realities**

Plokhy sees three possible outcomes to the war:

1. **Military victory for Ukraine** – This would require continued Western support and a successful counteroffensive.
2. **Russian victory** – This would involve full annexation of Ukraine, including the eastern and southern regions.
3. **Stalemate and compromise** – This would involve a negotiated settlement, but Plokhy doubts this is possible.

The main obstacle is the Russian constitution. In 2022, Putin included five Ukrainian regions—Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson, and Crimea—into the Russian constitution. This is not symbolic. It is a legal barrier.

Any peace agreement that returns to pre-2022 borders would require a constitutional change. That is politically impossible under the current regime.

Thus, a return to 2022 borders is not viable without a political change in Moscow.

The war has already lasted over two years. The front lines have not shifted dramatically. But Ukraine has achieved one major victory: the Black Sea. Ukrainian forces have pushed the Russian navy west, restored the grain corridor, and allowed 75% of pre-war exports to resume.

This is not a victory in the traditional sense. But it is a strategic success.

### **11. The Human Cost and the Role of Leadership**

Zelenskyy’s decision to stay in Kyiv during the invasion was not just political—it was symbolic. He was not a soldier. He was a comedian who had played a president on TV. But he became a real one.

Plokhy describes him as a leader who "feels the audience." He understood that his presence would inspire unity.

When Western leaders urged him to flee, he refused. He knew that if he left, the resistance would collapse.

His popularity is not just a measure of approval. It is a reflection of national unity. In a country divided by east-west tensions, Zelenskyy united Ukrainians.

He is not a perfect leader. But he is a real one.

---

### **12. Conclusion: History, Memory, and the Future**

The war in Ukraine is not just about territory. It is about memory. It is about who gets to write history.

The Soviet Union was not destroyed by external pressure. It was dismantled from within—by economic failure, by nationalism, by the desire for self-determination.

Ukraine’s story is not a simple one. It is complex, contradictory, and painful. But it is also powerful.

Plokhy warns that history is not over. The world is returning to a bipolar structure, but now it is Washington versus Beijing.

The Cold War is back. But it is not the same. The stakes are higher. The weapons are deadlier.

The lesson of the Cuban Missile Crisis was not luck. It was leadership. It was the fear of nuclear war. It was the recognition that no one wins.

That lesson must be remembered.
